## LinkedIn — Variant 1 (Contrarian hook)

Everyone I talk to reacts the same way when they hear about our hiring process: "How do you expect people to sign up for that?"

Six hours. Multiple rounds. Six different people involved. A CV screen, a call about who you are as a person before anything technical, a free-format ninety-minute technical conversation, and an assessment where you actually build something.

Most companies look at that and shorten it. Fewer rounds, faster offers, less friction in a tight market.

I get it. But shortening the process does not remove the need to filter candidates for cultural fit, technical depth and motivation. It just moves that filtering to after the hire, where it shows up as attrition, mismatched expectations, and trust that has to be rebuilt three months in instead of established on day one.

A high drop-off rate in hiring is not a broken funnel. Done right, it is doing exactly the job it is supposed to do.

## LinkedIn — Variant 2 (Story format)

A candidate once told me he did not want to go through our hiring process. Too long, too much time, too many people involved. He wanted an offer, not an odyssey.

I understood him. I still would not change a thing.

Our process runs six hours across multiple rounds: a CV screen, an introduction call that is entirely about who you are as a person, a free-format technical conversation with two experts, and an assessment where you build and present something real. Six people give up hours of their time. Candidates give up an afternoon or more, with no guarantee of a yes at the end.

A lot of people walk away. That is not a flaw. Once someone is in, nobody doubts whether they belong, because everyone went through the same six hours to get there. Our consultants talk about it openly with clients, and clients notice the difference.

The investment is real on both sides. So is the payoff.

## LinkedIn — Variant 3 (Question format)

Would you walk away from a job because the hiring process asked too much of your time?

One candidate did, with us. Six hours across multiple rounds felt like too big an ask, and he said so directly. I did not disagree with him. But I also did not shorten the process, because that six hours is doing something a two-round interview loop cannot: it filters for cultural fit, technical depth and genuine motivation, all before anyone signs an offer letter.

Shorter processes feel candidate-friendly. But the filtering they skip does not disappear, it just relocates to the first few months on the job, where it shows up as mismatches, attrition, or a new hire who never quite fit the way the team expected.

So here is the real question: would you rather filter before the hire, or discover the mismatch after it?
